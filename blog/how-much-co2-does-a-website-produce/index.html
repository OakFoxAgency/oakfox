How Much CO₂ Does a Website Produce? What 100 UK Audits Say Is Normal

The average UK small-business homepage emits about 0.24 g of CO₂e per view - around 29 kg a year at modest traffic. Real figures from our 100-site audit: what's normal, what's good, and what changes the number.

The honest answer to “how much CO₂ does a website produce?” is it depends on two numbers - how heavy each page view is, and how many views you get. But “it depends” is a cop-out without data, so here are the actual figures from our July 2026 audit of 100 UK small-business homepages, measured with the same Sustainable Web Design Model used by most carbon tools.

Per page view: the honest range

  • The median UK small-business homepage: 0.24 g of CO₂e per view - a D on the A+ to F scale, transferring 1.8 MB of data.
  • The global median page is around 0.36 g (an E). Half the web does better, half worse.
  • A well-built page: under 0.08 g (an A) - and under 0.04 g is A+ territory, the top 5% worldwide. Eighteen of our 99 sites managed it, so this is not an elite standard.
  • The extremes in one national sample: 0.003 g to 2.8 g per view. The heaviest homepage emitted nearly a thousand times more than the lightest, per visit, for the same job - being a small business’s front door.

Per year: multiply by your traffic

Grams per view only mean something once traffic is applied. Using the study’s median (0.24 g):

Monthly viewsCO₂e per year (median page)CO₂e per year (heavy page, 1 g/view)
1,000~3 kg~12 kg
10,000~29 kg~120 kg
100,000~290 kg~1.2 tonnes

For scale: 29 kg is roughly 65 miles in a petrol car; 1.2 tonnes is in the territory of a transatlantic flight seat. A single popular page on a heavy site can quietly out-emit a business’s entire paper use - and unlike most footprint lines, this one is invisible until someone measures it.

Two things follow from the table. First, most small-business websites are a minor line in the company footprint - worth fixing, not worth panicking over. Second, the ratio is the scandal: the heavy column is the same business reaching the same customers, just with an unconsidered web page. This is the cheapest emissions reduction most companies will ever find, because the fix also makes the site faster.

What actually drives the number

From the study’s byte-by-byte breakdown of everything those 100 pages transferred:

  • Images: 60.5% of all bytes. Not video, not JavaScript - photography, uploaded at camera size. 44 of 99 sites shipped over 1 MB of images on the homepage alone.
  • Video and media: 16% - mostly autoplaying backgrounds nobody asked for.
  • JavaScript: 10% - the villain of most performance writing turns out to be a supporting actor on small-business sites.
  • Fonts: 10% - a quiet one; 43 sites shipped over 200 KB of web fonts, most of which never render a visible character.
  • Hosting doesn’t change the bytes but changes the emissions per byte: verified renewable infrastructure meaningfully cuts the per-view figure - though not by enough to rescue a heavy page.

Common questions

How much CO2 does an average website produce per year?

It depends on page weight and traffic. The median UK small-business homepage in our July 2026 audit emitted about 0.24 g of CO₂e per view - at 10,000 views a month that's roughly 29 kg a year, about the same as driving 65 miles in a petrol car. The heaviest site we measured emitted 2.8 g per view, which at the same traffic would be over 330 kg a year. Same traffic, ten times the footprint - the difference is entirely the page.

What is a good carbon footprint for a website?

Under 0.08 g of CO₂e per page view puts you in the top 10% of pages tested worldwide (an A rating on the Sustainable Web Design scale); under 0.04 g is A+, the top 5%. The global median is around 0.36 g. For context, 18 of the 99 UK small-business sites in our study achieved an A or A+ - it's very attainable for an ordinary business site.

How is website carbon calculated?

Most tools, ours included, use the Sustainable Web Design Model: measure how much data one page view transfers, then convert those bytes to energy across data centres, networks, and end-user devices, and apply grid carbon intensity to get grams of CO₂e per view. It's a modelled estimate rather than a meter reading - accurate enough to compare sites fairly and to track whether changes work.

What makes a website's carbon footprint high?

Data transfer, overwhelmingly. In our 100-site audit, images accounted for 60% of every byte transferred - usually photography uploaded at camera size and never compressed. Video, JavaScript widgets, and web fonts make up most of the rest. Fossil-fuelled hosting raises emissions per byte; heavy pages raise the bytes. The heaviest homepage we measured was 22.6 MB; the lightest was 19 KB.
