Most UK small businesses do need a website, but there's a narrow strip of cases where a Google Business Profile or a single social page is genuinely enough. The short answer: if you're a limited company, or if any part of selling or booking happens online, you need a website. If you're a sole trader who's found by walking past and your customers already have your phone number, a free Google Business Profile often does more work than a half-finished site ever will.

We say that as a studio that also builds for tradespeople, shops and therapists, not only for authors and software firms. If the right answer for you turns out to be "not yet", we'd rather you spent the money on a van sign or a better camera for your phone than on a website you don't need.

Here are the five questions that settle it.

## 1. Is the Business a Limited Company?

If yes, you need something at your own domain. The GOV.UK guidance on [limited company signs, stationery and promotional material](https://www.gov.uk/running-a-limited-company/signs-stationery-and-promotional-material) is explicit that websites (along with letters and order forms) must display the company's registered number, its registered office address, where the company is registered and the fact that it is a limited company. There's no requirement to *have* a website, but there's a requirement for the website to carry those details *if* you have one, and in practice most limited companies find it easier to put up a one-page site than to keep rebutting the "do they actually exist?" question that customers increasingly ask of sole social-media presences.

A one-page site with company details, services, a phone number and a privacy notice is enough to satisfy the rule and reassure a customer. It isn't a big project.

## 2. Does Anything About the Business Happen Online?

If a customer ever needs to book a slot, pay a deposit, download a menu, join a waiting list, enquire about a job, see prices or read a care instruction, that's online activity. A page on a platform you don't own can carry some of it (a Facebook event, a Linktree, an Instagram DM), but it's clumsy and fragile.

A website is where the online-happening parts of your business live. The moment you find yourself explaining the same thing to the same question in DMs, that content wants a page.

We saw this with [Jennifer Lane](/portfolio/jennifer-lane), where five books, a podcast, Reiki sessions and a newsletter had outgrown a single social presence. The refreshed site gave each one a clear path, and the book launch had somewhere to send traffic. That's the shape of the rule: the moment the business does more than one thing online, a site earns its keep.

## 3. Would a Google Business Profile Do Instead?

A free Google Business Profile is the single best move for almost any UK business that serves local customers. It appears on Google Maps and in Search, carries your opening hours, phone number, address, photos and reviews, and lets customers message you directly. Setting one up is free and takes about an hour, with [verification via a short phone call, email or postcard](https://support.google.com/business/answer/2911778).

The catch Google is explicit about: a Business Profile is only for businesses that have face-to-face contact with customers. A fully remote online shop can't use one. Neither can a web-only service. For everyone else (a joiner, a hairdresser, a cafe, a physiotherapist, a nursery, a solicitor with walk-in appointments), a profile is doing work that a bad website can't match.

So: if you qualify, set the profile up first, then decide whether you also need a website for the deeper stuff. Often the answer is "yes but smaller than you thought".

## 4. Where Will Customers Try to Find You?

Watch what people actually type into Google. "Plumber Ormskirk." "Independent bookshop Southport." "Accessibility audit UK." If your best customers would type a *what* plus a *where* to find you, you need pages about those things on a domain you control, because that's where Google sends people. A social post rarely ranks for a service-plus-location search; a short, well-written page nearly always does.

If, instead, your customers find you through word of mouth, local papers or a physical sign on a shop door, the picture changes. A website still helps, but it's catching customers once they've already decided to look you up, not finding them in the first place.

Our shop clients, like [The Magpie's Tale](/portfolio/the-magpies-tale) in Churchtown, show the hybrid: most shoppers come in because they've walked past, but the website carries events, new arrivals and opening hours for the ones who looked them up afterwards. Both channels do their part.

## 5. Who Owns the Audience You're Building?

This is the quiet one. Every follower you win on a social platform is a tenant of that platform's algorithm. Meta can change reach rules, suspend a page, hide your posts, or charge you to show them to the audience you built. We've seen a decade-old Instagram presence throttled to a few hundred impressions a post after one policy change.

A website, a newsletter list and a Google Business Profile are different: you control the content on the site, the email addresses are yours, and the profile data belongs to the business. If the business matters to you long term, you want some of it on ground you own.

The honest version is that most small businesses need both. Social for reach, a website for ownership, a Business Profile for local discovery. Trying to do one and not the others is where things get brittle.

## So Do You Need One, or Not?

Three short answers, matched to the three shapes of business we see most often.

1. **Limited company with any UK customers.** Yes, a website. Even a one-pager with company details, services and a contact form is better than nothing.
2. **Sole trader with mostly-local, mostly-word-of-mouth customers.** Not yet. Set up a Google Business Profile, pick one social platform you'll keep up, and revisit in six months.
3. **Any business selling or booking anything online.** Yes, a website. The platform-only route breaks in the first busy week.

If you're not sure which you are, the fastest check is to search your business name on Google and on Maps. If you have a verified Business Profile, decent reviews and a social presence that comes up first, you can probably delay the site. If any of those are missing, start there before anything else.
